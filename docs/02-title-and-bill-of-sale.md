# 02 · Title and Bill of Sale

**Goal of this chapter:** confirm you can actually transfer ownership, and have all paperwork ready before a buyer ever appears. Time: ~1 hour, possibly spread over a week if paperwork is missing.

## Step 1 — Find the title (10 min)

The title (sometimes "certificate of title," pink in some states) is the single most important document. Common hiding places: a fireproof box, the folder from when you bought the car, or a safe-deposit box (which is fine — just go get it *now*, before sale day).

**Do not keep the title in the glovebox.** If the car is stolen or someone rummages at a test drive, a loose title is a gift to a fraudster.

If you can't find it: order a duplicate from your state DMV (usually $10–$30, by mail or online). This can take 1–3 weeks — this is exactly why this chapter comes before you list the car.

## Step 2 — Check the title for problems (15 min)

Look at the title itself and answer:

- [ ] **Is it in your name?** If it's still in a deceased relative's name or has never been transferred to you, extra steps (probate, transfer forms) apply — check your DMV site *now*.
- [ ] **Any lien listed?** If you financed the car, the lender may hold the title (or be listed as lienholder). You must satisfy the payoff before you can transfer clean title. Ask your lender for a payoff quote and their release process — this can take days.
- [ ] **Is it "clean" or branded?** Salvage, rebuilt, flood, or fleet branding is *disclosed on the title itself* and must be disclosed to buyers. Branded cars sell for much less — price accordingly.
- [ ] **Any liens/mistakes?** If a co-owner's name is on it, they must sign too. If your name is misspelled, fix it with the DMV before selling.
- [ ] **Back of title blank?** That's where the transfer happens on sale day (see below).

## Step 3 — Understand the back of the title

Almost every state's title has a transfer section on the back containing, roughly:

1. **Odometer reading** at time of sale — federal law requires this; write actual miles
2. **Seller's signature and printed name** — exactly as it appears on the front
3. **Buyer's name and address**
4. **Date of sale and sale price**
5. **Notarization** — required in ~half the states (AZ, CA no; PA, OH, LA yes — check yours!). If notarization is required, sign the title **in front of** a notary: do not sign it in advance.

Big rule: **use blue or black ink, no cross-outs, no white-out.** Mistakes can void the title and force a duplicate from the DMV. If unsure of the exact boxes, take the *unsigned* title to your bank or AAA — they often notarize free and know the drill.

## Step 4 — Prepare the bill of sale

Many states don't *require* one, but you want one anyway. It protects you ("as-is, no warranty") and gives both parties a receipt. Two options:

- Use your state DMV's official form if it has one (search "[your state] bill of sale DMV")
- Use the free template in [07 · Templates](07-templates.md) — print two copies, one for each of you

## Step 5 — Round out your document folder

- [ ] Title (found, verified, ready to sign)
- [ ] Current registration
- [ ] Maintenance records (a fat folder raises trust and offers)
- [ ] Both copies of the bill of sale, pre-filled except date/signature
- [ ] Both keys / fobs found (or be honest with buyers about the missing one)
- [ ] Owner's manual and any spare tire kit
- [ ] A bill of lading or transport plan if selling to a distant buyer

## Checklist — Paperwork (one sitting, ~1 hour)

- [ ] Found the physical title
- [ ] Title is in my name (or I've started the DMV fix)
- [ ] No liens — or I've called the lender about payoff
- [ ] Checked branding (salvage/flood/etc.) and noted it for disclosure
- [ ] Looked up whether my state requires title notarization
- [ ] Bill of sale drafted (two copies)
- [ ] Registration, records, owners manual, keys collected in one folder
