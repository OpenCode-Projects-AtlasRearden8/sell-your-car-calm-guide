Sell your car privately, without the panic.

Selling privately gets you $1,000–$3,000 more than a trade-in on a typical used car. The catch: you have to be the dealer — set the price, meet strangers, hold the paperwork, take the money. This guide breaks that into five small stages, each with a checklist sized for one sitting. No accounts, no tracking, no JavaScript required — it all works offline and prints cleanly.

1 Price your car fairly

One sitting, ~30 minutes

  1. Look up your car in three places. Kelley Blue Book (kbb.com), Edmunds, and your live local market (Facebook Marketplace, Craigslist, eBay sold listings). If two numbers are close and one is extreme, trust the two.
  2. Rate your car honestly. Very good (minor wear, everything works) vs. good (normal wear) vs. fair (needs repair work). The check-engine light you've been ignoring will be found — price it in now.
  3. Write down three prices. Ask price (market +5–10%, rounded to something clean like $7,900), target price (market value), walk-away price (market −10%). Do not renegotiate the walk-away with yourself on sale day.
  4. Compare against listings. Priced in the middle, your car sells in about a week. Top-third prices can take a month. Need it gone fast? Price low, sell the first weekend.
  5. Prep for photos. A $15 wash and vacuum, personal items out, windows clean. Skip the $200 detail unless the price bump is realistic.
Checklist — Pricing
  • Value checked on KBB, Edmunds, and live local listings
  • Honest condition rating chosen
  • Ask, target, and walk-away prices written down
  • Ask price compared to current local listings
  • Car washed, vacuumed, personal items removed
  • Title, registration, records, spare keys gathered

2 Gather the title and bill of sale

One sitting, ~1 hour (plus DMV lag if documents are missing — so do this before listing)

  1. Find the title. Fireproof box, purchase folder, safe-deposit box. Never keep it in the glovebox. Can't find it? Order a duplicate from your DMV now — it can take 1–3 weeks.
  2. Check it for problems. In your name only? No liens listed (if you financed it, call your lender about payoff)? Any branding (salvage, flood, rebuilt) that must be disclosed?
  3. Understand the back of the title. Sale day happens here: odometer reading (required by federal law), price, date, both signatures. Some states require notarization — sign only in front of the notary if so. Blue or black ink, no cross-outs.
  4. Prepare the bill of sale. Two copies, one for each of you. It's your "as-is, no warranty" protection.
  5. Build the sale folder. Title, registration, maintenance records, manual, both keys.
Checklist — Title & Paperwork
  • Physical title found
  • Title in my name (or fix started)
  • No liens — or lender payoff process started
  • Branding checked and noted for disclosure
  • State notarization requirement checked
  • Bill of sale drafted — 2 copies
  • Registration, records, manual, both keys in one folder

3 Run safe test drives

Per buyer, ~20 minutes prep

  1. Screen by phone first. Name, number, when they can come. Cancel anyone who won't give a number, wants you to drive to them, or pays via links/gift cards/mystery "escrow."
  2. Meet in public, in daylight. Best: a police-station safe-exchange lot. Not at night, not "somewhere the buyer picks," and never let a stranger into your home.
  3. Copy their license before the keys move. "I have to copy any test driver's license for insurance." Accurate, and nobody argues. Confirm with your insurer that permitted test drivers are covered (Stage 5 chapter covers the call).
  4. You drive first. A three-minute loop warms the car and shows it works. Then hand over, with a route: "the highway and back, about ten minutes." Ride along. Always.
  5. Nobody drives your car alone. Not "to their mechanic," not "back in 20 minutes." If their mechanic is real, meet the mechanic together at the shop.
  6. Walk the car afterward and tell the flaws first. Same story every time, before they find the scratch. Honesty kills negotiating ambushes.
Checklist — Safe Test Drives
  • Public daytime location chosen
  • Buyer's name and phone number collected
  • Driver's license copied before the drive
  • Insurer confirmed test-driver coverage
  • Title, wallet, valuables NOT in the car
  • I drive first; friend rides along if possible
  • Buyer never drives alone
  • Car re-locked; flaws pre-disclosed; no rule-breaking deposits

4 Get paid safely

Sale day — read before the buyer arrives

MethodVerdict
Cash, verified at your bank (teller counts + deposits)Best — free and bulletproof
Cashier's check printed at the buyer's bank, watched, then deposited at yours the same hourBest for large amounts
Bank wire, confirmed settled in your accountGood for large amounts
Zelle / Venmo / PayPal from a strangerReversible after fraud — deposit-size only
Personal check / emailed "escrow" link / gift cards / cryptoNever — these are the scam
  1. Money first, always. Verified, in your hands or your bank's hands, before anything is signed.
  2. "Available" is not "cleared." A fake cashier's check can be pulled from your account days later. Wait for full clearance before handing over the title.
  3. Overpayment is a scam, not a mistake. "$9,000 for the $7,000 car — just refund my $2,000." No. Cancel the deal.
  4. A screenshot of "I sent it" is not payment. Money in your account is payment. Nothing else is.
  5. Sign everything in one sitting: title back (mileage, price, date, both signatures) + bill of sale, both keeping copies. Photograph them.
  6. Remove your plates, hand over keys last.
Checklist — Payment Safety
  • Payment method agreed and vetted in advance
  • Cash counted at the bank, or teller-verified
  • Cashier's check watched being issued, or phone-verified (number I looked up)
  • Funds fully cleared before title changed hands
  • No overpayment accepted
  • Title back + bill of sale signed, copies photographed
  • Plates removed; keys handed over last

5 Notify the DMV and your insurer

Within 24 hours of the sale, ~30 minutes — this is the step people forget, until it costs them

  1. File your state's notice of sale (a.k.a. "release of liability" or "notice of transfer"). Search "[your state] DMV notice of sale" — most states do it online in 5 minutes, some by mail. Deadlines range from 5 to 30 days. Until this is filed, you are legally on the hook for the car — tickets, tolls, even accidents run by the new owner.
  2. Keep proof — screenshot the online confirmation, or send certified mail with return receipt. If a ticket ever appears, this is your shield.
  3. Call your insurer. Buying a replacement? Add the new car first, then drop the old one in the same call — no coverage gap. Otherwise just remove the old car and ask about the pro-rated refund of prepaid months.
  4. Loose ends: plates returned or transferred per state rules, toll transponder unlinked, subscriptions and auto-payments cancelled, complete sale folder kept for records.
Checklist — DMV & Insurer
  • State notice-of-sale form filed
  • Confirmation screenshot or receipt saved
  • Old car removed from policy (new car added first, if buying)
  • Pro-rated refund confirmed
  • Plates handled per state rules
  • Toll transponder and subscriptions cancelled
  • Sale folder kept for records

The three rules that keep this calm

  1. Slow is safe. Rushed buyers are a red flag. Real buyers wait.
  2. Paperwork before people. Confirm the title transfers before the car is listed.
  3. Not done until the DMV is told. Liability ends when your state gets its notice — not one second before.
